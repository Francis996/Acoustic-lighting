## Confirmed facts for the news entry

1. Acoustic Products published the article on September 25, 2026. Its page labels it as an article and identifies the author as “Administrator.” [Original article](https://www.acoustic-products.co.uk/an-acoustic-panel-is-not-a-neurodiversity-strategy/)
2. The manufacturer says neurodivergent users are not one group with one set of requirements and that there is no universally “neurodivergent-friendly” acoustic panel. [Original article](https://www.acoustic-products.co.uk/an-acoustic-panel-is-not-a-neurodiversity-strategy/)
3. It identifies BB93 as providing essential acoustic performance standards for school buildings, while arguing that an inclusive learning environment needs consideration beyond passing an acoustic test. [Original article](https://www.acoustic-products.co.uk/an-acoustic-panel-is-not-a-neurodiversity-strategy/)
4. The article says the UK Department for Education’s *Inclusive education estates* guidance identifies quiet spaces and sensory comfort/acoustics as core inclusive-design considerations. The cited official guidance includes “Quiet spaces and sensory comfort” as a core design element and says requirements vary across individuals. [Acoustic Products article](https://www.acoustic-products.co.uk/an-acoustic-panel-is-not-a-neurodiversity-strategy/) · [Department for Education guidance (PDF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a3bb3cf4c7605ab567238e7/inclusive-education-estates.pdf)
5. It says wall and ceiling panels can control reflected sound and reduce reverberation, with results depending on absorption characteristics, area, position, frequency response and construction behind the panel. [Original article](https://www.acoustic-products.co.uk/an-acoustic-panel-is-not-a-neurodiversity-strategy/)
6. The manufacturer distinguishes other problems that panels alone cannot resolve: sound through a partition may need improved separation; dominant mechanical-services noise needs quieter services; a busy dining hall may need zoning and quieter areas; and simultaneous activities may need a different layout. [Original article](https://www.acoustic-products.co.uk/an-acoustic-panel-is-not-a-neurodiversity-strategy/)

## Editorial guardrails

- This is **manufacturer-authored technical commentary**, not an independent study, a medical or neurodevelopmental authority, a product test report, a case study, or a new standard or regulation. Attribute its interpretation and recommendations to Acoustic Products.
- Do not claim a panel, acoustic-lighting product, BB93 compliance, or the article itself makes a space neuroinclusive, removes a barrier for every pupil, guarantees speech intelligibility, reduces distress, or delivers a health, learning, productivity, or regulatory outcome.
- Keep the source’s technical distinction clear: absorption addresses reflected sound/reverberation, whereas separation, mechanical noise, spatial zoning and activity layout may require other interventions. Never frame “more absorption” as a universal remedy.
- The publishable B2B takeaway is early, whole-environment coordination: establish the users and activities; target zones; room geometry; sound sources and pathways; acoustic criteria; finishes; partitions; HVAC; AV; lighting; access; maintenance; and verification responsibility before selecting any treatment.
- Acoustic lighting may be described only as a potentially coordinated ceiling element. It needs configuration-specific acoustic evidence, photometry, glare, electrical, suspension, fire/compliance and maintenance information, plus project-specific analysis; this article contains no acoustic-lighting product evidence.
