## Confirmed facts for the news entry

1. The peer-reviewed original research was published by *Frontiers in Built Environment* on September 25, 2026. The authors are Matteo Pellegatti, Chiara Visentin, Simone Torresin, Francesco Babich and Nicola Prodi, with affiliations including the University of Ferrara, University of Trento and Eurac Research. [Original article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2026.1869553/full)
2. The study compared a classroom-like laboratory experiment with a prior real-classroom experiment using the same student panel and calculation task. It included 189 Grade VII and VIII students, with a mean age of 12.8 years. [Original article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2026.1869553/full)
3. In the laboratory, the listening conditions were quiet, playground noise, birdsong, and mechanical ventilation at 160 L/s and 200 L/s. Participants were split between a better and poorer indoor-air-quality condition. [Original article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2026.1869553/full)
4. The authors report main effects for listening conditions, mediated strongly by individual noise sensitivity; their laboratory analysis found no combined acoustics-and-IAQ effect on calculation performance. [Original article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2026.1869553/full)
5. The laboratory and real-classroom results did not align: pupils were generally faster in the laboratory, and the authors describe the discrepancy as evidence of the “lab-dilemma.” They state that the precise causes were not established. [Original article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2026.1869553/full)
6. The authors say the lab rooms held only eight students, compared with classes of 14–26, which may have reduced peer distraction. They also identify the use of pure CO2 and the between-subjects design as possible contributors to the absence of an observed combined effect. [Original article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/built-environment/articles/10.3389/fbuil.2026.1869553/full)

## Editorial guardrails

- This is one peer-reviewed study in educational settings; it is **not** a workplace trial, a product evaluation, a lighting study, or a specification standard. Do not present its student findings as measured outcomes for offices, hospitality, healthcare or commercial interiors.
- Do not state that playground noise, birdsong, mechanical ventilation, any acoustic treatment, or acoustic lighting universally improves calculation, wellbeing, productivity or cognitive performance. The results varied by setting and individual noise sensitivity, and the authors explicitly caution against broad generalisation.
- The work does **not** prove that indoor air quality is unimportant. Its laboratory study did not observe a combined effect under its own conditions; the authors identify design limitations and call for further research.
- A supportable B2B takeaway is that acoustic, HVAC/ventilation, occupancy and activity conditions should be coordinated and validated in the actual space. For commercial projects, require project-specific acoustic criteria, HVAC noise data, commissioning/measurement scope and user/activity zoning rather than relying only on laboratory evidence or a product claim.
- Acoustic lighting may be positioned as one coordinated ceiling element only. Its absorption, photometry, glare, electrical, fire/compliance, suspension and maintenance requirements need product-specific documentation and project-specific design; the study evaluates none of these.
